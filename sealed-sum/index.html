Add two numbers you can't see

Your phone locks two numbers. A server adds them while they stay locked. Only your phone can open the answer. This is real encryption running in this tab, nothing is sent anywhere.

Same number, a new lock every time

If 1250 always locked to the same bytes, the server could just learn the pattern. So every lock mixes in a fresh random number. Press the button a few times and watch the strip change while the number underneath stays the same.

What the server actually did

There's no secret trick on the server. It multiplied the two locked numbers together. In this scheme, called Paillier after Pascal Paillier who published it in 1999, multiplying the locks adds the numbers inside them.

lock(a) × lock(b) mod n² = lock(a + b)

It can also multiply a locked number by a plain number it knows, which is enough for totals, averages and weighted sums. Try asking it to add 21% VAT to your locked a.

What happens if the server tries to peek

Say the server guesses a key. Here it builds its own key of the same size and uses it to open the locked sum.

The price of the lock

None of this is free. A small number becomes a big block of bytes, and the server's work grows fast with the key size. These numbers are measured in your browser right now.

What it can't do

Paillier can add, and it can multiply by numbers the server already knows. It can't multiply two locked numbers together, and it can't compare them. That's enough for a sum of invoices. It isn't enough to run an AI model, which needs both, millions of times.

Schemes that can do both are called fully homomorphic encryption. Craig Gentry showed the first one in 2009. They work today, at a much higher price than the one above. How much higher, and whether that's fine for something like masking names in a legal document, is what the rest of this series measures.

Everything runs in this page with JavaScript BigInt and your browser's random number generator. It's a teaching demo, not a crypto library, so don't use this code to protect real data. Paillier, P. (1999). Public-Key Cryptosystems Based on Composite Degree Residuosity Classes. EUROCRYPT '99.
